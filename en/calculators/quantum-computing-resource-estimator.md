faq:
  - question: How many qubits do I need for my quantum algorithm?
    answer: "Qubit requirements depend on your problem size and desired precision. Most practical applications need 50-1000 logical qubits, which translates to 50,000-1,000,000 physical qubits with error correction."
  - question: What's the difference between logical and physical qubits?
    answer: "Logical qubits are the 'perfect' qubits your algorithm uses. Physical qubits are the actual hardware qubits needed to implement one logical qubit with error correction - typically 100-10,000 physical qubits per logical qubit."
  - question: When will quantum computers be practical for real applications?
    answer: "Some applications like optimization and simulation may become practical in 5-10 years with 100-1000 logical qubits. Cryptographically relevant quantum computers may require 10-20 years and millions of physical qubits."
  - question: How do I choose between quantum hardware platforms?
    answer: "Superconducting qubits offer fast operations, trapped ions provide high fidelity, and photonics enable networking. Choose based on your algorithm's requirements for speed, accuracy, and connectivity."
  - question: What error rates are needed for practical quantum computing?
    answer: "For fault-tolerant quantum computing, physical qubit error rates need to be below 0.1-1% (the error correction threshold). Current systems have error rates of 0.1-1%, approaching this threshold."
  - question: Can I run quantum algorithms on classical computers?
    answer: "Yes, for small problems (up to ~30-40 qubits). Quantum simulators can model quantum algorithms classically, but they become exponentially slow as qubit count increases."
  - question: How much does quantum computing cost?
    answer: "Cloud quantum computing costs $0.001-$0.1 per quantum operation. Building quantum computers costs millions to billions of dollars. Cloud access is most cost-effective for research and development."
  - question: What programming languages are used for quantum computing?
    answer: "Popular quantum programming languages include Qiskit (Python), Cirq (Python), Q# (Microsoft), and OpenQASM. Most are based on Python for ease of use and integration with classical computing."
