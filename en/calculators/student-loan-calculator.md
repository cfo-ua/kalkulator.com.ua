    <h2>Student Loan Calculator - Plan Your Education Debt Repayment</h2>
    <p>Calculate your <strong>student loan payments</strong> and explore different repayment options. This calculator handles federal and private loans, income-driven repayment plans, and loan forgiveness scenarios.</p>

    <div style="background: #e8f4fd; border: 1px solid #bee5eb; border-radius: 0.5rem; padding: 1rem; margin: 1rem 0;">
      <p style="margin: 0; font-weight: 500; color: #0c5460;"><strong>💡 New Graduates:</strong> Use this calculator to understand your repayment options before your grace period ends. Federal loans typically have a 6-month grace period after graduation.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>Federal vs Private Student Loans:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Federal Loans:</strong> Fixed rates, income-driven plans, forgiveness programs, flexible deferment options</li>
      <li><strong>Private Loans:</strong> Variable or fixed rates, fewer repayment options, credit-based approval</li>
      <li><strong>Direct Subsidized:</strong> No interest while in school (undergraduate, financial need)</li>
      <li><strong>Direct Unsubsidized:</strong> Interest accrues during school (graduate and undergraduate)</li>
      <li><strong>PLUS Loans:</strong> Higher limits for parents and graduate students</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Federal Repayment Plans:</h3>
    <p><strong>Standard Repayment (10 years):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Fixed monthly payments</li>
      <li>Highest monthly payment, lowest total interest</li>
      <li>Default plan for most federal loans</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Extended Repayment (25 years):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Lower monthly payments</li>
      <li>Available if you have $30,000+ in loans</li>
      <li>More total interest paid</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Graduated Repayment (10 years):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Payments start low and increase every 2 years</li>
      <li>Good for expected income growth</li>
      <li>More interest than standard plan</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Income-Driven Repayment Plans:</h3>
    <p><strong>Income-Based Repayment (IBR):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>10-15% of discretionary income</li>
      <li>Payment cap at standard 10-year amount</li>
      <li>Forgiveness after 20-25 years</li>
      <li>Must show partial financial hardship</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Pay As You Earn (PAYE):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>10% of discretionary income</li>
      <li>Payment cap at standard 10-year amount</li>
      <li>Forgiveness after 20 years</li>
      <li>Must be new borrower as of Oct 1, 2007</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Revised Pay As You Earn (REPAYE):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>10% of discretionary income</li>
      <li>No payment cap</li>
      <li>Forgiveness after 20 years (undergraduate) or 25 years (graduate)</li>
      <li>No financial hardship requirement</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Income-Contingent Repayment (ICR):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>20% of discretionary income or fixed 12-year payment</li>
      <li>Available for all federal loan types</li>
      <li>Forgiveness after 25 years</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Loan Forgiveness Programs:</h3>
    <p><strong>Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Forgiveness after 120 qualifying payments</li>
      <li>Must work for qualifying government or non-profit employer</li>
      <li>Must be on income-driven repayment plan</li>
      <li>Only Direct Loans qualify</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Teacher Loan Forgiveness:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Up to $17,500 forgiveness after 5 years</li>
      <li>Must teach in low-income schools</li>
      <li>Math, science, and special education teachers get higher amounts</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Federal Student Loan Interest Rates:</h3>
    <p><em>Note: Interest rates are set annually. Check the U.S. Department of Education website for current rates.</em></p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Undergraduate Federal:</strong> 5.50% fixed (example rate)</li>
      <li><strong>Graduate Federal:</strong> 7.05% fixed (example rate)</li>
      <li><strong>PLUS Loans:</strong> 8.05% fixed (example rate)</li>
      <li><strong>Private Loans:</strong> 4.00% - 14.00% (variable or fixed, market-dependent)</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Strategies to Reduce Student Loan Costs:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Make payments during grace period:</strong> Reduce principal before interest capitalizes</li>
      <li><strong>Pay more than minimum:</strong> Apply extra payments to principal</li>
      <li><strong>Autopay discount:</strong> Many lenders offer 0.25% rate reduction</li>
      <li><strong>Tax deductions:</strong> Deduct up to $2,500 in student loan interest</li>
      <li><strong>Employer assistance:</strong> Some employers offer student loan repayment benefits</li>
      <li><strong>Refinancing:</strong> Consider private refinancing for better rates (lose federal benefits)</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>What to Do if You Can't Make Payments:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Deferment:</strong> Temporary pause for qualifying situations (school, unemployment)</li>
      <li><strong>Forbearance:</strong> Temporary pause for financial hardship</li>
      <li><strong>Income-driven plans:</strong> Reduce payments based on income</li>
      <li><strong>Loan consolidation:</strong> Combine multiple federal loans into one</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Private Student Loan Considerations:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Credit requirements:</strong> Often need cosigner for best rates</li>
      <li><strong>Variable rates:</strong> Can increase over time</li>
      <li><strong>Fewer protections:</strong> Limited deferment and forbearance options</li>
      <li><strong>No forgiveness:</strong> Generally no forgiveness programs available</li>
      <li><strong>Refinancing options:</strong> Can refinance for better terms</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Understanding your repayment options early can save thousands in interest and help you become debt-free faster. Use this calculator to compare different scenarios and choose the best strategy for your situation.</p>

faq:
  - question: "Should I pay off student loans early?"
    answer: "Consider your interest rate vs. other investment opportunities. If your loans have high rates (7%+), paying extra can save significant interest. If rates are low (3-4%), you might invest the extra money instead."
  - question: "What happens to my student loans if I die?"
    answer: "Federal student loans are discharged upon death. Private loan policies vary - some discharge the debt, others may require the estate or cosigner to pay. Check your loan terms."
  - question: "Can I switch repayment plans?"
    answer: "Yes, you can change federal loan repayment plans at any time by contacting your loan servicer. Some plans have eligibility requirements or may require annual income verification."
  - question: "Should I consolidate my student loans?"
    answer: "Federal consolidation can simplify payments and make you eligible for certain forgiveness programs. However, you may lose borrower benefits and the new rate is the weighted average of existing rates."
  - question: "Is student loan refinancing worth it?"
    answer: "Refinancing can lower your rate but you'll lose federal benefits like income-driven plans and forgiveness programs. Only consider if you have stable income and don't need federal protections."
  - question: "How does student loan interest capitalization work?"
    answer: "Unpaid interest gets added to your principal balance during certain events (end of deferment, leaving income-driven plans). This increases your total debt and future interest calculations."
  - question: "What's the difference between subsidized and unsubsidized loans?"
    answer: "Subsidized loans don't accrue interest while you're in school at least half-time. Unsubsidized loans accrue interest from the time they're disbursed, even during school."
  - question: "Can my student loans be forgiven in bankruptcy?"
    answer: "Student loans are generally not dischargeable in bankruptcy unless you can prove 'undue hardship' - a very difficult legal standard to meet. This applies to both federal and private loans."
