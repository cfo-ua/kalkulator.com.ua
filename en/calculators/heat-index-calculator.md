    <h2>Heat Index Calculator - Know Your Heat Risk</h2>
    <p>Calculate the <strong>heat index (apparent temperature)</strong> based on air temperature and relative humidity. Get personalized heat safety recommendations and understand your risk of heat-related illness.</p>

    <div style="background: #fff3cd; border: 1px solid #ffeaa7; border-radius: 0.5rem; padding: 1rem; margin: 1rem 0;">
      <p style="margin: 0; font-weight: 500; color: #856404;"><strong>⚠️ Heat Safety:</strong> The heat index shows how hot it really feels when humidity is combined with temperature. High heat index values can be dangerous to human health.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>What is Heat Index?</h3>
    <p>The heat index, also known as apparent temperature, combines air temperature and relative humidity to determine how hot it actually feels. High humidity prevents sweat from evaporating efficiently, making your body feel hotter than the actual air temperature.</p>

    <h3>Heat Index Categories:</h3>
    <p><strong>80-90°F (27-32°C) - Caution:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Fatigue possible with prolonged exposure and physical activity</li>
      <li>Stay hydrated and take breaks in shade</li>
      <li>Limit strenuous outdoor activities</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>90-105°F (32-41°C) - Extreme Caution:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Heat cramps and heat exhaustion possible</li>
      <li>Avoid prolonged exposure to sun</li>
      <li>Increase water intake significantly</li>
      <li>Wear light-colored, loose-fitting clothing</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>105-130°F (41-54°C) - Danger:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Heat cramps and heat exhaustion likely</li>
      <li>Heat stroke possible with prolonged exposure</li>
      <li>Minimize outdoor activities</li>
      <li>Seek air conditioning</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Above 130°F (54°C) - Extreme Danger:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Heat stroke and heat exhaustion highly likely</li>
      <li>Avoid all outdoor activities</li>
      <li>Stay in air-conditioned spaces</li>
      <li>Seek immediate medical attention for symptoms</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Heat-Related Illnesses:</h3>
    <p><strong>Heat Exhaustion Symptoms:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Heavy sweating or no sweating</li>
      <li>Muscle cramps</li>
      <li>Fatigue and weakness</li>
      <li>Dizziness and headache</li>
      <li>Nausea and vomiting</li>
      <li>Cool, moist skin</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Heat Stroke Symptoms (EMERGENCY):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>High body temperature (104°F+)</li>
      <li>Hot, dry skin (or profuse sweating)</li>
      <li>Rapid pulse</li>
      <li>Confusion or altered mental state</li>
      <li>Loss of consciousness</li>
      <li>Call 911 immediately</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Prevention Strategies:</h3>
    <p><strong>Clothing and Gear:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Light-colored, loose-fitting, breathable clothing</li>
      <li>Wide-brimmed hat and sunglasses</li>
      <li>Sunscreen (SPF 30+)</li>
      <li>Cooling towels or vests for workers</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Hydration Guidelines:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Drink water before you feel thirsty</li>
      <li>8-12 ounces every 15-20 minutes during activity</li>
      <li>Avoid alcohol and caffeine</li>
      <li>Include electrolyte replacement for prolonged sweating</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Activity Modifications:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li>Schedule activities for cooler parts of the day</li>
      <li>Take frequent breaks in shade or AC</li>
      <li>Reduce intensity and duration of outdoor work</li>
      <li>Use the buddy system to watch for heat illness</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>High-Risk Populations:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Children:</strong> Less efficient temperature regulation</li>
      <li><strong>Adults 65+:</strong> Decreased ability to adapt to heat</li>
      <li><strong>Chronic conditions:</strong> Heart disease, diabetes, kidney disease</li>
      <li><strong>Medications:</strong> Diuretics, beta-blockers, antipsychotics</li>
      <li><strong>Pregnant women:</strong> Increased metabolic demands</li>
      <li><strong>Outdoor workers:</strong> Prolonged heat exposure</li>
      <li><strong>Athletes:</strong> High exertion in heat</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Workplace Heat Safety:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Acclimatization:</strong> Gradual exposure over 7-14 days</li>
      <li><strong>Work/Rest cycles:</strong> Mandatory breaks in cool areas</li>
      <li><strong>Training:</strong> Recognize heat illness symptoms</li>
      <li><strong>Emergency plan:</strong> Procedures for heat emergencies</li>
      <li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Regular temperature and symptom checks</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Indoor Heat Safety:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Air conditioning:</strong> Most effective cooling method</li>
      <li><strong>Fans:</strong> Create air movement, less effective at high temperatures</li>
      <li><strong>Cool zones:</strong> Libraries, malls, community centers</li>
      <li><strong>Insulation:</strong> Block heat from entering home</li>
      <li><strong>Window coverings:</strong> Reduce solar heat gain</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Global Warming and Heat:</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Increasing frequency:</strong> More extreme heat days annually</li>
      <li><strong>Urban heat islands:</strong> Cities 2-5°F warmer than rural areas</li>
      <li><strong>Nighttime temperatures:</strong> Less cooling overnight</li>
      <li><strong>Humidity trends:</strong> Warmer air holds more moisture</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Use this calculator to assess heat risk and plan appropriate safety measures for outdoor activities, work, and daily life during hot weather conditions.</p>

faq:
  - question: "How is heat index different from temperature?"
    answer: "Heat index combines temperature and humidity to show how hot it actually feels. High humidity prevents sweat evaporation, making you feel hotter than the air temperature alone."
  - question: "At what heat index should I avoid outdoor activities?"
    answer: "Exercise extreme caution when heat index reaches 105°F (41°C) and avoid prolonged outdoor activities above 130°F (54°C). Listen to local heat advisories and your body's signals."
  - question: "Why is humidity important for heat safety?"
    answer: "Humidity affects your body's ability to cool itself through sweating. High humidity prevents sweat from evaporating, reducing your body's natural cooling mechanism and making heat more dangerous."
  - question: "How much water should I drink in hot weather?"
    answer: "Drink 8-12 ounces of water every 15-20 minutes during activity in heat. Start hydrating before you feel thirsty and continue drinking even after activity ends."
  - question: "What should I do if someone has heat exhaustion?"
    answer: "Move them to a cool place, remove excess clothing, apply cool water to skin, provide small sips of water if conscious, and seek medical attention. For heat stroke, call 911 immediately."
  - question: "Can fans help when it's very hot?"
    answer: "Fans become less effective when air temperature exceeds skin temperature (about 95°F). Above 99°F, fans may actually increase heat stress by blowing hot air."
  - question: "How long does it take to acclimate to heat?"
    answer: "Heat acclimatization typically takes 7-14 days of gradual exposure. Workers new to hot environments should start with shorter periods and gradually increase exposure time."
  - question: "Are some people more susceptible to heat illness?"
    answer: "Yes, children, adults over 65, people with chronic medical conditions, those taking certain medications, and individuals who are not physically fit are at higher risk for heat-related illness."
